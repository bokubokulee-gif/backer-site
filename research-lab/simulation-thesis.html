Attention simulation / Thesis

A plausible person is not yet a predictive population.

Model how individual decisions become collective outcomes. Test the resulting distribution against observed behavior.

Backer Research20 September 2026Research note · 01

Research thesis · The preview contains fictional agents. Predictive accuracy requires separate evaluation.

01

Believability and prediction ask different questions.

A simulated person can remember a conversation, follow a plan and explain a choice. These behaviors make a world legible. A forecasting claim asks something else: whether the distribution of choices matches what a defined population does under stated conditions.

Generative Agents joins memory, reflection and planning to produce believable social behavior. That architecture is a research starting point. Its believability evaluations are not a calibration study of purchases, votes or trades.

02

Grounding gives the model an empirical target.

A demographic prompt creates a description. An interview or behavioral record supplies observations that can constrain a representation. The current self-report grounding preprint tests agents against held-out responses from the people represented, giving the representation a defined empirical target.

Behavioral fine-tuning research similarly evaluates learned response distributions on social-science experiments. The important distinction is the existence of a held-out task and comparator. A model that succeeds there still needs new evidence for a different population, incentive or time period.

03

Interactions are valuable when they change a testable result.

Individual decisions can alter the conditions faced by others. A share changes exposure; a recommendation changes what a colleague considers; an order can change available liquidity. A multi-agent model is useful when these feedbacks improve a specified prediction beyond a model that treats people independently.

Central proposition

Represent an interaction when it changes a measurable outcome, and retain it only when that change survives evaluation.

For Backer, a first test is an ablation: hold the initial population and information constant, remove peer transmission or memory, and compare held-out predictive error. If performance does not deteriorate, the removed mechanism has not demonstrated value. If performance improves, the added mechanism may be noise.

04

Counterfactuals require more than a second run.

A baseline and an alternative can differ inside a program without identifying what a real intervention would do. Observational records confound exposure with preference: people who already care may seek more information and act more often. Replaying the same correlation can make a simulation look causal.

The proposed standard is to evaluate changes against experiments or other defensible intervention evidence. Prespecify treatment, population, outcome and horizon. Test whether the simulator preserves both average effects and important heterogeneity; do not infer success from matching the untreated total alone.

05

Confidence requires a model of error.

An agent can offer a fluent explanation and remain wrong. A population can agree because all agents share the same blind spot. Simile’s confidence work treats expected error as a separate object to predict and evaluate. That is a more demanding claim than consistency across repeated generations.

Backer should earn comparable claims through prospective evidence. Until then, a simulation can still expose assumptions, compare mechanisms and identify which observations would change a decision. It should make the path from attention to memory, choice and allocation inspectable—while leaving its predictive status explicit.
